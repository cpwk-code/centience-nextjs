The position you're in

Your advisors are adopting AI faster than any approval process can move. Note-takers, assistants and general-purpose models — bought individually, on personal subscriptions, sometimes on accounts that don't carry your domain.

You can see that it's happening. What you can't see is what's going into those tools, what comes back, or what an AI agent did on its own. There are no logs to review, so there is no supervision to document — and "we have a policy" is not an answer to that.

The instinct is to archive everything and review it. That instinct creates two new problems: a body of records you can later be compelled to produce, and a supervisory standard you have implicitly promised to meet on every item.

There is a better-established answer, and your firm already uses it for email.

What we do

We centralize AI access, capture what passes through it, and turn that into a risk-ranked review queue — the same shape as lexicon-based communications supervision, applied to a channel that currently has none.

01
Ingest. AI interactions across approved tools, collected centrally rather than left on individual advisor accounts.
02
Risk-rank. Scored against lexicons and data types you define — client PII, account numbers, trade and suitability language, advisors on heightened supervision. Not everything surfaces. That is the point.
03
Flag autonomous activity. Where an AI agent acted without a human in the loop, so it can be reviewed as a category rather than discovered later.
04
Route and evidence the review. Who reviewed what, on what sampling basis, on what cadence, with what disposition. The artifact is the record that supervision occurred.
05
Redact before submission. PII and firm-defined sensitive categories removed in real time, before they reach a model, rather than audited afterwards.
06
Retain on your terms. A 30-day auto-delete is as supported as a seven-year hold. See overleaf.

Two things we will say plainly

Scope supervision to what you will actually operate

A firm that commits to reviewing every AI interaction has created an examinable obligation, and will eventually be measured against the one it missed. A defined, risk-ranked sample with a documented methodology is the stronger position — and it is the one examiners already understand from communications review. We would rather build a narrow program you can prove completely than a broad one you can only describe.

Retention is your decision, not our default

Whether AI prompts constitute required records is genuinely unsettled, and there is a real argument for keeping the window short. The platform supports a 30-day auto-delete as readily as a seven-year hold. We will give you the tradeoff honestly and configure whichever way your counsel and CCO land — and we will not manufacture a recordkeeping obligation to enlarge our scope.

The third-party exposure most diligence misses

A model provider's SOC 2 report and a no-training commitment are meaningful — and they are not the whole picture. Those commitments sit on top of a sub-processor chain most firms have never enumerated, and a provider's own policy language often preserves more latitude than the summary suggests.

This matters because a breach anywhere in that chain lands as your notification obligation, on your clock, under Regulation S-P — which has applied to firms of every size since 3 June 2026. You cannot assess a dependency you have not mapped. We maintain the vendor and sub-processor register alongside the supervision.

What has to be true first

Centralized access. None of this works against tools bought individually by advisors on personal accounts. There has to be one point of management to connect to.

That does not mean issuing corporate devices or mandating a single stack. You can stay technology-agnostic about which tools advisors use while still requiring that approved tools run through firm-held tenancy — sequenced, with the tools already in widest use first.

Before the engagement starts we tell you which of your controls are enforceable in software, which are verifiable but not enforceable, and which come down to attestation. Labelling the third as the first is how programs fail examinations.

Where we fit
